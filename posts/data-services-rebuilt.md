For decades, services have been sold by the hour. A company hires a partner, then another, and another. Each one learns on the client's time until the budget runs out. By then the project is stuck, and the platform is often the easiest thing to blame.

In most cases, nobody acted in bad faith. The model made it happen. When you are paid for hours, the hours are the product. Learning on the client's time is billable. Nobody owns the outcome. Data services have worked this way too.

That is changing. AI is reshaping both how services are sold and how they are delivered. Where the technology is headed is becoming clear. The harder question is what it means for the companies that rely on those services.

## Services are being rebuilt

Services have always been hard to scale. Margins are thin. The work is manual. To grow, you hire.

AI breaks the link between growth and headcount. As we wrote when [we introduced Mure](/blog/introducing-mure), [services are becoming the new software](https://sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software/). New AI-native firms in insurance, legal, and accounting are not selling tools to incumbents. They are rebuilding the firm, with AI at the center.

The examples are multiplying. One entrepreneur [bought a traditional accounting firm and exposed it through MCP](https://x.com/AlbertSalgueda/status/2120545112145211423), changing how it operates.
The metric changes too, from headcount to revenue per employee. Being small used to be a constraint. Now it is an advantage. A small team amplified by agents can do what used to take a large one.

New roles are appearing. The [forward-deployed engineer (FDE) is the fastest-growing engineering role right now](https://x.com/lennysan/status/2120547244953031043). Hyperscalers and AI labs alike are investing in forward deployment. Microsoft's [Frontier Company](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/frontier-company) brings its engineers alongside client teams, OpenAI has set up its own [deployment company](https://deploy.co/), and Anthropic works with partners such as [Ode](https://www.ode.com/). Value is moving to the people who understand business problems and deploy systems in real-world environments. Even for the leading labs, models alone are not enough. They need the people who put them to work.

But every vendor starts from its own stack. A lab's engineers deploy that lab's models, a platform's engineers build on that platform, and what the problem actually needs comes second. Not every task needs the largest model. For many, a smaller or open one is enough. The same holds in data: for some scenarios, a single platform or engine is all a company needs. That is the role of independent FDEs, tied to no single model or platform: start from the problem, choose what is best for the client, and stay with them as the stack changes. Their work complements the vendors rather than competing with them.

## The same is happening in data

Since the 1990s, data has gone through many eras: data warehouses, big data, cloud warehouses, the modern data stack, open table formats and lakehouses, and now enterprise context, agents, and the catalog wars. New engines, products, libraries, companies, and labs appear every year. A client who waited six months for results will not wait six months again when results can come in weeks. [One of our clients](https://muredata.com/#customers), a professional football club, now builds apps connected to its lakehouse in days instead of weeks.

Yet the services model has barely changed. Teams are large: engagement manager, project manager, solution architect, data consultant, analytics consultant, director, you name it. Contracts are time and materials or fixed fee, both priced in hours. Every change becomes a change request. AI makes this model harder to defend. Under time and materials, an hour an agent saves is an hour no longer billed. Under a fixed fee, it becomes margin. Either way, the client pays for effort and rarely sees the gain.

The market, though, is already moving. Large firms are acquiring specialized data teams: [Deloitte bought Wavicle Data Solutions](https://dealroom.co/news/147897-deloitte-buys-wavicle-to-boost-its-databricks-and-ai-engineering-muscle/), [CDW acquired Lovelytics](https://investor.cdw.com/news/news-details/2026/CDW-to-Acquire-Lovelytics-to-Turn-Customer-Data-into-Business-Value/default.aspx), [IG&H took over RevoData from Atomic Group](https://www.igh.com/post/ig-h-neemt-revodata-over-van-atomic-group), [Tryolabs joined Qubika](https://tryolabs.com/newsroom/qubika-acquires-tryolabs), and [Avanade acquired Plain Concepts](https://www.plainconcepts.com/avanade-to-acquire-plain-concepts/), to name a few. They are buying specialist expertise, not more capacity.

There is an obvious objection. If AI does the work, why not let the client do it? Some will, with data teams of their own, large or small. Many have no data team at all. And building a data system, putting it into production, and running it takes expertise and direction.

With the right guidance, AI multiplies expertise. Without it, AI produces the wrong thing, only faster.

Writing code was never the hard part. Understanding the business problem and knowing what to build for it was. [As Berkeley researchers argue on the BAIR blog, if intelligence becomes virtually free, the work shifts to building data systems for, of, and by agents](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2026/07/07/intelligence-is-free-now-what/).

Data is more important than ever. How can every company put to work what only it owns: its data and IP?

## Your data is the frontier

Frontier models are available to everyone. A company's data and know-how are not.

[The Reverse Information Paradox](https://snscratchpad.com/posts/reverse-information-paradox/) frames the stakes. In the past, the seller risked giving knowledge away in order to sell it. Now, "the buyer risks giving away knowledge, just in order to use what they bought." Models learn from the prompts people write, the tools agents use, and the corrections people make, and those corrections carry a company's institutional know-how. In the cloud era, "enterprises accumulated data." In the AI era, they accumulate learning.

That know-how lives in two places. The first is live data, which changes continuously: operational systems, lakehouses, warehouses, and agent traces. The second is tacit knowledge: what a company has learned over years and rarely writes down, its "company brain." The challenge is making both available across departments, securely, so that people and agents can act on them.

The race has started. Anthropic now has [Claude Data](https://claude.com/resources/articles/sheets-whiteboards-data), and OpenAI has a [data agent in ChatGPT Work](https://openai.com/index/put-data-to-work/). Databricks, Google, Microsoft, and Snowflake are all competing for the enterprise context layer. Most offer MCP connectors and skills to plug into your systems. Connectors are becoming a commodity. Context is not: it is specific to each company, and it can only be built close to the business.

## The shifts ahead

Models and data platforms have never been more capable. The bottleneck is no longer what AI can do or what platforms can offer, but what organizations can absorb: how they bring AI, data, and expertise together and put them to work on the problems that matter to their business. That gap is where data services will prove their value. Here is some of what we still need to get right:

1. **From timesheets to results.** Services have always priced effort. When agents compress weeks into hours, effort stops measuring value. What matters is not what an hour costs, but what the client can now achieve. That calls for a new equilibrium between client and firm: a small fixed fee that commits both sides, and a variable success fee tied to goals we set with the client before the work begins. If the goals are met, the fee is paid. It is that simple. Retainer models are shifting too, from paying for availability to paying for results.

2. **From pyramids to people.** The traditional model ran on a pyramid of roles. As agents absorb the execution, those roles collapse into fewer, broader ones. Data is following the FDE: real, experienced engineers who carry a problem from the first business conversation to production. What makes them valuable is what agents cannot provide: craft, responsibility, and agency when AI falls short.

3. **From one-offs to compounding.** Delivery needs a new way of working. Projects were one-offs: each started from zero, and its lessons left with the team. A system carries them forward, for our own teams and for our clients, from the first call to production and beyond. Skills, MCP servers, and agents are building blocks everyone has. What compounds is the know-how: what worked, what broke, what was worth building. Each project makes the next one faster, and speed wins.

4. **From spectators to builders.** Much of the conversation is still about the latest model or data platform: benchmarks, feature comparisons, launch announcements. But the frontier does not only advance inside labs and vendors. It advances inside every company that puts its own data and know-how to work. That is where we want to build: new products, research, and experiments, one company at a time. That is why we are creating Labs at Mure Data.

This is what we're building. We are still in the opening miles of a marathon. Much remains unpredictable, but we are excited about what comes next.
