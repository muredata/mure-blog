For decades, services have been sold by the hour. A company hires a partner, then another, and another. Each one learns on the client's time until the budget runs out. By then the project is stuck, and the platform is sometimes the easiest thing to blame.

In most cases, nobody acted in bad faith. The model made it happen. When you are paid for hours, the hours are the product. Learning on the client's time is billable. Nobody owns the outcome. Data services have worked this way too.

That is changing. AI is reshaping both the "business" and the "engineering" of services. Where the technology is headed is becoming clear. The harder question is what it means for the companies that rely on those services.

## Services are being rebuilt

AI breaks the link between growth and headcount. As we wrote when [we introduced Mure](/blog/introducing-mure), [services are becoming the new software](https://sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software/). New AI-native firms in insurance, legal, and accounting are not selling tools to incumbents. They are rebuilding the firm, with AI at the center.

The examples are multiplying. One entrepreneur [bought a traditional accounting firm and exposed it through MCP](https://x.com/AlbertSalgueda/status/2120545112145211423), changing how it operates. Y Combinator teaches [how to build an AI-native services company](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Rk-how-to-build-an-ai-native-services-company).

The metric changes too, from headcount to revenue per employee. Being small used to be a constraint. Could it now be an advantage? A small team amplified by agents can do what used to take a large one.

Speed changes with it. A client who waited six months for results will not wait six months again when the work can be done in weeks. For example, one of our clients in the sports industry now ships data apps in days, not weeks.

New roles are appearing too. The [forward-deployed engineer (FDE) is the fastest-growing engineering role right now](https://x.com/lennysan/status/2120547244953031043). Hyperscalers and AI labs alike are investing in forward deployment. Microsoft's [Frontier Company](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/frontier-company) brings its engineers alongside client teams, OpenAI has set up its own [deployment company](https://deploy.co/), and Anthropic works with partners such as [Ode](https://www.ode.com/). Value is moving to the people who understand business problems and deploy systems in real-world environments. Even for the leading labs, models alone are not enough. They need the people who put them to work.

But every vendor starts from its own stack, and not every problem needs the largest model or the most feature-rich data platform. Independent FDEs, tied to no single model or platform, start from the problem and choose what is best for the client.

## The same is happening in data

Since the 1990s, data has gone through many eras: data warehouses, big data, cloud warehouses, the modern data stack, open table formats and lakehouses, and now enterprise context, agents, and the catalog wars. New engines, products, libraries, companies, and labs appear every year.

Yet the services model in data has barely changed. Teams are large: engagement manager, project manager, solution architect, data consultant, analytics consultant, director, you name it. Contracts are long, time and materials or fixed fee, both priced in hours. Every change becomes a change request. AI makes this model harder to defend every day.

The market is already moving. Large firms are acquiring specialized data teams: [Deloitte bought Wavicle Data Solutions](https://dealroom.co/news/147897-deloitte-buys-wavicle-to-boost-its-databricks-and-ai-engineering-muscle/), [CDW acquired Lovelytics](https://investor.cdw.com/news/news-details/2026/CDW-to-Acquire-Lovelytics-to-Turn-Customer-Data-into-Business-Value/default.aspx), [IG&H took over RevoData from Atomic Group](https://www.igh.com/post/ig-h-neemt-revodata-over-van-atomic-group), [Tryolabs joined Qubika](https://tryolabs.com/newsroom/qubika-acquires-tryolabs), and [Avanade acquired Plain Concepts](https://www.plainconcepts.com/avanade-to-acquire-plain-concepts/), to name a few. They are buying specialist expertise, not more capacity.

There is an obvious objection: if AI does the work, why pay for expertise at all? Why not let the client do it? Some will, with data teams of their own, large or small. Many have no data team at all. And building a data system, putting it into production, and running it takes expertise and direction. With the right guidance, AI multiplies expertise. Without it, AI produces the wrong thing, only faster.

Writing code was never the hard part. Understanding the business problem and knowing what and how to build for it was. [As Berkeley researchers argue on the BAIR blog, if intelligence becomes virtually free, the work shifts to building data systems for, of, and by agents](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2026/07/07/intelligence-is-free-now-what/).

Data is becoming more important than ever. How can every company put to work what only it owns: its data and IP?

## Where the value is

Frontier models are available to everyone. A company's data and know-how are not.

[The Reverse Information Paradox](https://snscratchpad.com/posts/reverse-information-paradox/) frames the stakes. In the past, the seller risked giving knowledge away in order to sell it. Now, "the buyer risks giving away knowledge, just in order to use what they bought." Models learn from the prompts people write, the tools agents use, and the corrections people make, and those corrections carry a company's institutional know-how. In the cloud era, "enterprises accumulated data." In the AI era, they accumulate learning.

That know-how lives in two places. The first is live data, which changes continuously: operational databases, lakehouses, agent traces, and more. The second is tacit knowledge: what a company has learned over years and rarely writes down, its "company brain," along with its connection to the company's tools. The challenge is making both available across departments, securely, so that people and agents can act on them.

The race has started. Anthropic now has [Claude Data](https://claude.com/resources/articles/sheets-whiteboards-data), and OpenAI has a [data agent in ChatGPT Work](https://openai.com/index/put-data-to-work/). Databricks, Google, Microsoft, and Snowflake are all competing for the enterprise context layer. Most offer MCP connectors and skills to plug into your systems. Connectors are becoming a commodity. Your data is not: it is specific to each company.

## The shifts ahead

Models and data platforms have never been more capable. The bottleneck is no longer what AI can do or what platforms can offer, but what organizations can absorb: how they bring AI, data, and expertise together and put them to work on the problems that matter to their business. That is where data services will prove their value. Here is some of what we still need to get right:

1. **From timesheets to results.** Services have always priced effort. When agents compress weeks into hours, effort stops measuring value. What matters is what the client achieves. That calls for a new equilibrium: a small fixed fee that commits both sides, and a variable success fee tied to goals agreed with the client upfront. Goals met, fee paid. Retainer models are shifting too.

2. **From pyramids to people.** The traditional model ran on a pyramid of roles. As agents absorb the execution, those roles collapse into fewer, broader ones. Data is following the FDE: real, experienced engineers who carry a problem from the first business conversation to production. What makes them valuable is what agents cannot provide: craft, responsibility, and agency when AI falls short.

3. **From projects to systems.** Delivery needs a new way of working. Projects were one-offs: each started from zero, and its lessons left with the team. A system carries them forward, for our own teams and for our clients, from the first call to production and beyond. Skills, MCP servers, and agents are building blocks everyone has. What carries over is the know-how: what worked, what broke, what was worth building. Speed compounds.

4. **From clients to products.** A services firm sees what few others can: the same problems repeating across clients, industries, and stacks. In the old model, those patterns were often forgotten. Now we can experiment quickly and build products, one company at a time. Services are also labs.

Everyone has access to the same models. Only you have your data. That is where the frontier is.
